Standard investment asset classes

Stocks (equities)
- High risk, but historically outperform most other assets over long periods

Bonds (fixed income)
- Low risk, less volatility than stocks but subject to interest rate and credit risks.

Foreign currencies
- Betting on changes in exchange rates between currencies
- High volatility, influenced by global events, interest rates and economic data
- Used for short-term trading or speculation

Commodities
- Physical goods
- Prices volatile due to supply-demand shocks

Real estate
- Risk depends on location, market cycles and liquidity
- Used for diversification and long-term wealth building

Bonds
- principal amount/face value/par value
- coupon rate, interest rate
	- zero bonds have no coupons, but a "premium" paid at maturity
- time-to-maturity
- present value of bond is the sum of the present value of all future cash flows (this holds for all fixed income instruments)
- yield-to-maturity (YTM) - the internal rate of return earned by an investor who buys a bond today at market price
	- assumes bond held to maturity and the cashflows are paid on time
	- YTM is often solved for numerically
- bond price is inversely proportional to market interest rate
	- i.e. present value is inversely proportional to r, the interest rate
	- lower market interest rate -> higher value of bond
	- this is why bonds are subject to interest rate risk, because their value is affected by changing interest rates on the market
- Macaulay duration - a measure of how long it takes for the price of a bond to be repaid by the cash flows from it
	- for zero-bonds, the Macaulay duration is equal to the time-to-maturity
	- Macaulay duration, T = - dPV/dr * 1/PV
	- bonds with longer time-to-maturity are more sensitive to interest rate risk
- Investors prefer bonds with longer maturity when interest rates are expected to fall
	- this might be the mechanism which leads to yield-curve inversion: more demand for long-running bonds pushes the yield curve down at the long-end and up at the short-end -> leads to an inversion
- Investors prefer bonds with shorter maturity when interest rates are expected to rise
- Risk associated with bonds:
	- interest rate risk: changing interest rates on the market affect the value of a bond
	- default risk: the risk that the bond issuer is unable to make principle and coupon payments on time
	- inflation risk: the value of cash flows may be affected due to inflation
- Bonds and monetary policy:
	- central banks buy and sells bonds to control the supply of money in the economy
	- Open Market Operations (OMOs) - the buying and selling of government bonds by the central bank in the open market
		- if the central bank buys bonds from commercial banks, the commercial banks will have more cash available
			- this means lending is cheaper -> interest rates fall (spending is more attractive, inflation rises)
		- if the central bank sells bonds to the commercial banks, the cash reserves of the commercial banks reduces. This means that lending is more expensive -> there should be less money available in the economy, slowing down lending/spending (thus, slowing inflation)
- Current yield - the annual coupon payment as a fraction of the bond's price
- Stocks vs. bonds
	- stocks are shares in the ownership of a business
	- bonds are a form of debt that the issuing entity promises to repay at some point in the future
	- stockholders have voting rights, bond holders do not
	- bonds have no dividends
	- bonds are seen as safer investments
- Pricing bonds:
	- bonds must be priced using the market interest rate to account for the opportunity cost of investing money elsewhere at that prevailing rate.
	- the market interest rate reflects the current cost of capital.

Modern portfolio theory (Markowitz model):
- Mean = E[x] = \mu	("expected return")
- Variance = E[(x-\mu)^2] = Var(x) = std(x)^2	("risk")
- Covariance = E[(x-\mu_x)(y-\mu_y)] = Cov(x,y)
- Correlation = Cov(x,y) / (std(x) * std(y))
	- a dimensionless measure of how two variables move together
	- for stocks in a portfolio, you want to have as little correlation as possible between the values
- Modern portfolio theory first formulated by Harry Markowitz in 1952
	- a model for portfolio optimisation in terms of expected return (mean) and risk (variance)
	- known as "Markowitz model"
- Portfolio: a collection of financial investments such as stocks, bonds, commodities and cash
- Idea: combine multiple assets in order to reduce risk as much as possible ("diversification")
- Assumptions of Markowitz model:
	1) asset returns are normally distributed
	2) more risk equals more return
- An efficient portfolio is a portfolio that has the highest return for a given level of risk or the lowest risk for a given return
- Does not account for short positions?
- Log daily returns (logarithm of daily returns).
	- Used because of mathematical convenience
- Key portfolio variables: 
	- asset weights
	- asset returns (historical data is used)
	- asset expected returns (calculated as average of returns)
- Expected return of a portfolio is the weighted average of the expected returns of the constituent assets
	- scalar product between weight vector and mean vector (each element corresponds to a single asset)
- Risk of portfolio is measured by the volatility, which can be approximated by the standard deviation of the variance
- Sharpe ratio is the ratio of the excess return you receive for taking on excess volatility which you endure by taking on a riskier asset
- The optimal portfolio has the highest Sharpe ratio
- Optimal portfolio lies on the Capital Market Line (CML). This line is tangential to the optimal portfolio on a return-risk diagram. To the left of the intersection, you have the "lending" side, where you have diluted your risky asset with a risk-free asset, lowering the expected return. To the right of the intersection, we have the "borrowing" side where you gain additional return by borrowing risk-free assets to invest more in the risky asset (the portfolio is "leveraged")

Capital Asset Pricing Model (CAPM)

- Diversification cannot eliminate all risk

Types of risks in finance:
- unsystematic (specific) risk: the component of a stock's return that is not correlated with market movements.
	- Diversification reduces unsystematic risk!
- systematic (market) risk: this risk cannot be diversified away. This risk arises due to interest rate changes, recessions or wars. The CAPM model measures this risk with the \beta parameter.

- CAPM was formulated by William Sharpe in the early 1960s
- Expected return r_a of an investment: 
	E[r_a] = r_f + \beta_a * (E[r_m] - r_f)
r_f: risk-free return
r_m: market return (e.g. S&P 500 index)
E[r_m] - r_f: "market premium"
\beta_a: multiplication factor

\beta_a = Cov(r_a, r_m) / Var(r_m): defines how risky your portfolio is relative to the market
It measures the stock's relative volatility compared to the rest of the market.
\beta_a = 1: stock moves exactly as market
\beta_a < 1: stock is less volatile than the market, expect a lower return
\beta_a > 1: stock is more volatile than the market, expect a higher return
\beta_a = 0: the stock has no systematic risk (i.e. risk-free)
A portfolio's \beta is the weighted sum of the \beta values of the stocks in the portfolio.

\beta_a is determined using linear regression.
\beta_a measures the systematic risk. \alpha is the intercept of the linear function, which is assumed to be zero in CAPM.

Derivatives

- Financial contracts whose value depends on the price of an underlying asset. It's value depends on fluctuations in the value of the underlying asset.
- Very flexible/creative - there can be derivatives for any financial asset (commodities, currencies, stocks, bonds, real-estate - e.g. credit derivatives)
- Useful for hedging risk and for speculation (i.e. making profit)
- Forwards/futures - contract between parties to buy something at a specified date at a specified price. Forwards are OTC. Futures are ETD.
	- Futures involve daily settlement, forwards do not (?)
		- Ah, because futures have to be cleared. That involves a variation margin.

Leverage

- The use of borrowed money or financial instruments to create large exposure with a relatively small initial investment, amplifying both gains and losses.
- Trader has to pay margin and financing costs associated with the borrowed capital.
- Collateral - assets pledge to secure your obligations. This may be cash, securities, crypto or other approved assets.
	- A margin is a type of collateral. It is a portion of collateral that brokers require to open a leveraged position and which absorbs day-to-day losses.
	- Initial margin - the initial collateral posted as the position is opened
	- Maintenance margin - the minimum margin which must be on the margin account
		- If margin account falls below maintenance margin, there is a "margin call".
- Derivatives are leveraged assets.

Forward contracts

- unlike options, these are obligations to buy/sell at the pre-agreed price
- Forward Rate Agreements - lock-in an interest rate for a future time period (i.e. lock-in a forward-rate)

Swaps

- Main types: interest rate swap, currency (FX) swap, credit default swaps
- FX swap:
	- exchange two currencies at today's rate (spot)
	- reverse the exchange at a future date (forward) at a pre-agreed rate
	- two legs: spot transaction, forward transaction
	- FX forward rates depend on interest rates in the two currencies

- Credit Default Swaps can be used to hedge bond default risk
	- you own a bond
	- you own a CDS for protection from default of the issuer of the bond
	- if nothing happens, you receive the bond coupons and pay the CDS premium
	- if the default happens, the bond's value drops sharply but the CDS pays the loss amount. 
		- so the default loss is offset

Options

- Like futures contracts EXCEPT there is no obligation to trade -- the person who is long on the option has the right to trade
- Non-linear valuation function
- A short call option is NOT a put option
	- an investor who shorts a call pockets the premium, but has to sell the underlying to the counterparty a the strike price if they exercise their right to buy
- Long Call: right to buy
- Long Put: right to sell
- The potential loss of short options is unbounded (!)
	- infinite loss!
- American option: may be exercised at any time before the expiration date
- European option: may be exercises only at a single pre-defined point in time, which is the expiry date
- Put-Call Parity:
	- portfolio 1: long call, short put
		- if stock price is above or below the strike price, the net payoff is the same
	- portfolio 2: long stock, borrow present value of strike price
		- has identical payoffs to portfolio 1
	- *** since portfolio 1 and 2 have the same payoff at expiration, their present values must be equal ***
	- This means that if we know the value of a call option, we can calculate the value of the put optioon for the same underlying, strike price and expiry date
- The price of an option is calculated using the Black-Scholes Model

Random behaviour in Finance

- Fundamental analysis looks at a companies assets, liabilities and earning reports and so on to find the value of a company
- A powerful tool in the fundamental analysis of a company's performance is the price-to-earnings ratio
- Price-to-Earnings (P/E) ratio:
    PE = Share price / Earnings per share
    Earnings per share = Net Income / Number of Shares
- Technical analysis focuses instead on historical price changes in stock. The price of the stock reflects the value of the company
    - they look for specific patterns in the historical data
- Quantitative analysis tries to model the behaviour of financial assets

- Daily price returns approximately follow a normal distribution (though, bear in mind the fat-tails - more of a Cauchy distribution)
- Quantitative analysis models returns as random variables drawn from some kind of distribution
- Stock price developments can be modelled as a random walk (Wiener process)

Wiener Process and Random Walk
- a continuous-time stochastic process with independent, normally-distributed increments
- assumption: the return of a given stock price is normally distributed
- assumption: each increment is independent of the one before
- assumption: stock prices follow a log-normal distribution -- i.e. the logarithm of X is normally-distributed
- Wiener process assumes changes in price are normally distributed, with mean zero and variance dt
- Random walk equation: stochastic differential equation
- dS = \mu S dt + \sigma S dW
    - dS = change in Stock price
    - dt = time step (deterministic part)
    - dW = change in Wiener process W(t+dt) - W(t) ~ N(0,dt) (stochastic part)
    - S = current stock price

The Black-Scholes Model and the Greeks

- solution of the Black-Scholes model is the price of an option at a given time
- The Delta - the rate of change of the option price with respect to the price of the underlying
- The Gamma - the rate of change of the Delta with respect to the price of the underlying
- The Theta - the rate of change of the option price with respect to time
- The Vega  - the rate of change of the option price with respect to volatility

- historical volatility - the volatility of an option's price based off historical returns
- implied volatility - the volatility of an option's price which ensures that the option price from the Black-Scholes model matches the market price
    - we take the price to be equal to the market price and solve for the volatility from the BS equation
    - implied volatility is often described as the "market sentiment" about future uncertainty
    - rule of thumb: increasing volatility leads to higher option values -> more uncertainty in the value of the underlying makes options on that underlying more valuable
- BS model assumes a constant implied volatility (this disagrees with what we see in the markets in general)
- The CBOE Volatility Index (VIX) is a real-time index that represents the market's expectations for the relative strength of near-term price changes of the S&P 500 index
    - VIX is calculated using historical and implied volatilities from option prices

Monte-Carlo simulations
- a method used to model uncertainty by running many random simulations of possible future outcomes
- it solves a deterministic problem using probabilistic methods
- can be used to predict stock price movements by running multiple random walk simulations (Wiener process)
    - we know the solution for a stock's price from Ito's lemma of the underlying stochastic differential equation

Option pricing with Monte Carlo simulations
- assumption: the underlying asset follows a geometric random walk, governed by a stochastic differential equation
- Wiener process is a random walk with mean 0 and variance t
- the mu-drift (deterministic part of the SDE) is given by the risk-free rate

Option trading strategies
- Option trading is about volatility - buying an option is only worth it when we expect large changes in price of underlying
- Straddle strategy: long call and put with same expiry and strike price.
    - called the straddle strategy because the PnL diagram as a function of S(T) is a saddle (finite loss for small changes in underlying prie, big gains for large changes in underlying price)

Value-at-Risk (VaR)
 - higher risk = higher return (conclusion of Modern Portfolio Theory and CAPM)
    - beta parameter: how risky is the asset in comparison to the rest of the market?
 - The trainer demonstrated a method for calculating the VaR by using historical data and assuming that the returns follow a normal distribution.
    - In the first implementation, he calculated the mean and standard deviation of the normal distribution and simply read the corresponding quantile
    - In the second implementation, he used a Monte-Carlo simulation to build up a distribution of possible returns.
        - This involves the solution to the geometric random walk (solved using Ito's lemma - chain rule for SDE)
        - The solution for the stock price over time requires mean and standard deviation parameters.
            - To obtain these, he used historical data and again assumed that the data fits a normal distribution

Collateralised Debt Obligations (CDOs)
- Commercial banks can issue and approve several types of loans
- As part of managing their risk appetite, they sell these loans on to investment banks
- Investment banks turn these loans into a complex product called a CDO
    - they sell these to investors (e.g. institutional investors) seeking higher returns than treasury bills and bonds
- Institutional investors are responsible for investing large pools of money on behalf of others
    - examples: pension funds, university endowments, insurance companies, mutual funds, hedge funds
- Retail investors are individuals investing their own money
- High net-worth investors/Family Offices are another category of investor
- Sovereign wealth funds
- A CDO gives the owner all cash flows associated with that product
    - sensitive to credit risk
    - a good CDO will contain loans which are uncorrelated (diverisification)
        - keep correlations close to zero
- CDOs are classified into different "tranches", which essentially means different levels of credit risk
    - more risky loans will give higher returns
- The demand for more CDOs potentially led to the creation of sub-prime mortgages
    - essentially, mortgages granted to individuals with poor credit scores which would normally not qualify for a conventional mortgage
- A high amount of defaulting, correlated sub-prime mortgages fuelled the global financial crisis (GFC)

Interest rate modelling
- goal is to predict the future value of interest rates
- interest rates are believed to be "mean-reverting processes", in contrast to geometric random walks which increase steadily over time (modelling of stocks)
- Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process (Vasicek model)
    - a stochastic process WITHOUT A DRIFT
    - instead, values fluctuate around a mean ("mean-reversion")
- The Vasicek model is a a simple model for interest rates with small time-to-maturity (i.e. for short-term lending)
    - effects bonds, mortgages and credit derivatives
    - influenced by political decisions, economic states (these we cannot model with Vasicek - too complex)
    - Vasicek model assumes that short-term interest rates follow a mean-reverting Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process

Long-term investing
- the opposite to algorithmic trading (buy-and-hold strategies)
    - algorithmic trading is about making money quickly using quantitative methods to outperform the market
- value investing: Benjamin Graham, Warren Buffet
    - looks for under-valued products to invest long-term in
    - tends to invest in companies, not stocks (using fundamental analysis of assets, liabilities and earning sheets etc)
    - value investors decide whether to buy a stock by looking at the relationship between the value of the stock and its current price
- efficient market hypothesis: "it is impossible to beat the market"
    - assumption: all available information about a company/product is already included in its stock price
